# 12 — Resource Vault: The 5 Protocols

A protocol is different from a checklist: a checklist gets a *task* done, a protocol gets *you* through a predictable recurring state. These five cover the five states that do the most damage, and all of them are predictable — which means all of them can have a plan written in advance, on a good day, by you, for you.

Each becomes a row in `DB: Resources`, `Type` = Protocol.

---

## P1 · The Dread Task Protocol
- **Use when:** "there's one task I've been avoiding for weeks and it's poisoning everything"
- **Topic:** Work · **Minutes:** 25 · **Energy:** 🔋 Medium

> **What a dread task actually is.** It is never the work. The work is almost always boring and ordinary. What you're avoiding is one of five things, and until you know which one, you'll keep applying the wrong fix — which is why "just do it" has failed you forty times on this specific task.

**Step 1 — Name the real obstacle (3 min).** Which one is it? Be honest; it's usually obvious the moment you're asked:

| It's this | The tell | The actual fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Ambiguity** | You don't know what "done" looks like | Define done in one sentence. That's today's whole task |
| **A hidden first step** | You know the task but not where it starts | Find the first *physical* action. Nothing else today |
| **Social cost** | Someone will be disappointed, annoyed, or know you're late | Write the message first, using the relevant script. The message *is* the task |
| **A possible bad answer** | Opening it might confirm something awful (a bill, a result, a reply) | Open it *with* someone, in person or on a call. Do not open it alone |
| **Shame about the delay** | The task is now 10% work and 90% "it's been nine weeks" | The apology is one line and nobody is counting. See S5 |

**Step 2 — Shrink it until it's boring (5 min)**
- [ ] Write the task as a verb and an object: *"email Sam about the invoice"*, not *"sort out the invoice situation"*
- [ ] Write the first **physical** action. Open what? Click what? Find what? If your answer has no verb like open, click, find, type or dial, it's still too abstract
- [ ] Shrink again. Then once more. Target: a step so small it's faintly embarrassing. *"Open the folder."* That's the task now. Genuinely
- [ ] If it's still frightening, the task you named isn't the real one. Go back to Step 1

**Step 3 — Borrow activation (2 min)**
- [ ] Book it into a body-doubling session, or get on a call with one person. **For a true dread task this is not optional** — the whole point is that you cannot generate activation for this one alone, and you've now proven that over several weeks
- [ ] Or: tell one person *"I'm doing [thing] in the next hour and I'll tell you when it's done."* Name a person, not the room

**Step 4 — Contain it (15 min)**
- [ ] Timer: 15 minutes. Phone in another room
- [ ] Do the embarrassing first step
- [ ] **When the timer goes you may stop, even if it's unfinished.** You almost certainly won't want to — dread tasks are usually far smaller than the dread, and once you're in, finishing is easy. That discovery is the actual point of the protocol
- [ ] Afterwards write down one line: **how long it really took.** You need this evidence for next time, because your brain will not believe it otherwise

**The thing to notice:** nine times out of ten the task takes less time than you spent dreading it that morning. Log it. Build the file of evidence. That file is what eventually shortens the dread phase from weeks to days.

## P2 · Time Multiplier Calibration
- **Use when:** "I'm always late and I always underestimate and I can't work out why"
- **Topic:** Work · **Minutes:** 5 to set up, 2 weeks to run · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **The finding:** adults with ADHD underestimate task duration consistently — commonly by a factor of two to three. Not randomly: *consistently*, which is excellent news, because a consistent error can be corrected with arithmetic. You don't need to get better at estimating. You need your personal multiplier and the discipline to apply it.
>
> **Why you estimate low.** You estimate from the memory of the best run, which was also the hyperfocused run, and you estimate the *work* while omitting the transitions — finding the file, opening the app, the four minutes of staring, the interruption, the recovery after the interruption. Transitions are where the time goes, and they're invisible in memory.

**Setup (5 min, once)**
- [ ] In your Desk, the `Est. (min)` and `Actual (min)` fields on Tasks
- [ ] For the next two weeks, on **every** task you do: write your gut guess *before*, and the real number *after*
- [ ] Don't try to be accurate. Guess the way you normally guess — a corrected guess ruins the data
- [ ] Minimum 5 tasks for a rough figure; 15 for a real one

**Reading your number**
Your Desk shows `total actual ÷ total estimated`. Typical results: **1.8 – 3.0**.
- **Under 1.5** — either you're already correcting, or you're only logging easy tasks
- **1.5 – 2.5** — the common range
- **Over 3** — usually means your tasks are too vague. Decompose them smaller and re-run

**Using it (the only part that matters)**
```
Gut guess × your multiplier + 15 minutes of transition = the real number
```
The flat 15 minutes isn't padding. It's the finding-the-file, opening-the-app, staring-at-it time that exists in every single task and appears in no estimate ever made.

**The three places to apply it immediately**
1. **Travel.** Leaving time, not arrival time. Arrival times have caused every late arrival of your life
2. **"I'll do it before X."** The single most dangerous sentence in time blindness. If the multiplied estimate doesn't fit before X, it doesn't happen before X
3. **Promising a date.** Multiply, then add a day. Asking once for longer costs you a fraction of what asking twice costs

**Recalibrate** every few months, and after any change to medication, job or sleep. The number moves.

**What counts as a win:** five logged tasks. Even a rough multiplier beats the instinct, which is wrong in the same direction every time.

## P3 · The 2pm Crash Protocol
- **Use when:** "it's the afternoon, I've done nothing, and I'm spiralling about it"
- **Topic:** Self-talk · **Minutes:** 10 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **What's happening is not a verdict.** For a lot of us the afternoon dip is some combination of medication wearing off, a genuine circadian trough, and the cumulative cost of four hours of masking and task-switching. It arrives at roughly the same time every day. A thing that happens on schedule is a feature of the machine, not a failure of character.
>
> **The real damage isn't the lost hours. It's the four hours of self-attack that follow**, which reliably cost more than the crash did.

**Right now (2 min — do these in order, don't skip to step 3)**
- [ ] **Eat something.** Anything. You have very likely not eaten enough today and have been attributing a blood-sugar event to a moral one
- [ ] **Drink water.** Same reason
- [ ] **Stand up and go outside for 90 seconds.** Not for wellness reasons — for the light and the temperature change, which does more for the next hour than any amount of resolve

**Then reset the day's definition (3 min)**
- [ ] Open **Bad Day Mode**
- [ ] Say the true sentence: *"The morning is gone. The afternoon is four hours and it is a separate thing."*
- [ ] Pick **one** low-energy, under-15-minute task. One. The temptation to salvage the day with a big push is what produces a second crash and a worse evening
- [ ] Do that one thing

**Then protect tomorrow (5 min, and this is the part that compounds)**
- [ ] Write tomorrow's **One Thing** now, while you know exactly what's unfinished
- [ ] Put the first physical action next to it
- [ ] Note the time of today's crash in your Energy Log

**The sentence to have ready**, because the self-attack arrives on schedule too:
> *"I'm not lazy, I'm at 2pm. Nothing is decided at 2pm."*

**The fix that actually ends this** (the structural one, over weeks)
- [ ] Log your energy daily for three weeks → find your real crash window
- [ ] **Stop scheduling anything cognitively demanding after it.** Not as surrender; as scheduling around a known constraint, the same way you'd schedule around a standing meeting
- [ ] Afternoons become admin, errands, laundry, low-stakes email, body doubling
- [ ] Move your genuinely hard work into your real good hours, which are usually earlier and shorter than you've been assuming

> Members consistently report this as the change with the biggest effect on both output and guilt: not working harder in the bad hours, but stopping filing the hard work into them.

## P4 · The Hyperfocus Exit Protocol
- **Use when:** "I disappear into things for 9 hours and come out having not eaten or moved"
- **Topic:** Health · **Minutes:** 5 to set up · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **Hyperfocus is not a superpower and it's not a choice.** It's an inability to disengage, and it's taken your dinner, your sleep, your appointments and sometimes a relationship. It can produce extraordinary work; it also produces a 3am stop, no food, and a wrecked next day — and the next day's cost is usually larger than the night's output.
>
> **You cannot exit hyperfocus from inside it.** Reasoning with yourself at hour six does not work, because the part that does the reasoning is the part that's engaged. So the exit has to be set up *before* you enter, by someone with a working brain — you, earlier.

**Set up before you start (2 min, every time — this is the whole protocol)**
- [ ] Decide the stop time and **say it out loud.** Out loud genuinely works better than thinking it
- [ ] **Two alarms:** one 30 minutes before stop, one at stop. Label them with the actual instruction: *"30 min — start landing"* and *"STOP — eat something"*
- [ ] Put food and a full glass of water **within arm's reach.** You will not get up. Accept that in advance and pre-position the food
- [ ] If you have an appointment later, set an alarm for leaving time × 1.5, **and** put something physical in your way — shoes on the keyboard, keys on the desk
- [ ] Tell one person your stop time if someone's around. Borrowed external structure works when internal structure doesn't

**The landing sequence, when the 30-minute alarm goes**
- [ ] Do not start anything new. This is the moment the whole protocol exists for: starting one more small thing at the 30-minute warning is how 9pm becomes 2am
- [ ] Write a **handoff note** to tomorrow-you: where you got to, the exact next action, where the file is. Three lines. This is what makes stopping tolerable — hyperfocus resists stopping partly because it fears losing the thread, and the note removes that fear
- [ ] Save, commit, close
- [ ] Stand up

**At the stop alarm**
- [ ] Eat the food. Drink the water. Both, before anything else
- [ ] Walk out of the room. Physically leave — transition needs a change of place, not a change of intention
- [ ] If you can't let go: set a hard cap of 20 more minutes with an alarm, then stop. A capped overrun is a vastly better outcome than an open-ended one

**The cost ledger, to read on a good day:** write down what hyperfocus has cost you — specifically. Missed meals, a missed flight, a missed birthday, three days of recovery. Keep it in your Desk. You won't read it mid-focus, but reading it on a Monday is what makes you set the alarms on Tuesday.

## P5 · The Sunday Dread Protocol
- **Use when:** "it's Sunday afternoon and the week ahead feels like a wall"
- **Topic:** Self-talk · **Minutes:** 20 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **Sunday dread is almost never about the work.** It's about *not knowing* — an unseen week containing an unknown number of unknown obligations, at least one of which you've forgotten. Ambiguity is the load, not workload. Which is why the fix is twenty minutes of making the week visible, and not a motivational reframe.

**Step 1 — Make it visible (5 min)**
- [ ] Open your calendar. **Next 7 days only.** Not the month; the month is a different, larger dread
- [ ] Count the fixed commitments. Say the number out loud: *"Four things. There are four things this week."* The number is nearly always smaller than the feeling
- [ ] Spot anything you'd forgotten → that's the actual source of the dread, and you just defused it

**Step 2 — Find the one hard thing (3 min)**
- [ ] One thing this week is heaviest. Name it in writing — the unnamed heavy thing taints every hour until it's named
- [ ] Write its first physical action next to it
- [ ] Decide which day it happens. **Pick the day, not the time** — times create a second failure point

**Step 3 — Subtract (5 min; the step people skip and the one that works)**
- [ ] What can you **cancel**? There's usually one thing. Cancel it now, tonight, with script **S3**
- [ ] What can you **postpone** without consequence? Move it
- [ ] What are you **not doing this week**? Write it down explicitly. A decided non-task stops costing you; an undecided one charges rent all week
- [ ] Target: remove one commitment entirely. One removal is worth more than any amount of planning

**Step 4 — Make Monday morning mechanical (5 min)**
- [ ] Monday's **One Thing**, written, with its first physical action
- [ ] Clothes out. Bag packed. Alarm set, with a leaving alarm as well as a waking one
- [ ] One nice thing in the week, placed on a specific day. Not a reward for performance — **just scheduled.** A week with nothing in it to look forward to is a worse week and that's a planning problem, not a personality one

**Step 5 — Close the day (2 min)**
- [ ] Shut the laptop. **No "quick look" at email.** A Sunday-night inbox glance can only ruin Sunday night; nothing in there can be fixed before Monday
- [ ] Say it: *"Four things. One of them is hard. It's on Wednesday. Monday's first action is written down."*

**What counts as a win:** Steps 1 and 3. Knowing the real number, and removing one thing. That's the difference between Sunday dread and Sunday evening.
