# 11 — Resource Vault: The 8 Checklists

**Design rules for every checklist here:**
1. **Timed, not open-ended.** "Tidy the flat" is infinite and therefore unstartable. "15 minutes, three bags" is finite and therefore startable. Every checklist has a timer and a stop condition.
2. **A named stop condition.** ADHD brains need a defined finish as much as a defined start, or the task either never begins or never ends (both happen, often in the same week).
3. **First item is always physical and absurdly small.** "Stand up." "Get a bag." Activation is the bottleneck, not the work.
4. **Explicit permission to do the partial version.** Every checklist states what counts as a win at 20% done, because 20% done is the realistic outcome and it must not register as failure.
5. **No step requires a decision you haven't already made for the member.**

Each becomes a row in `DB: Resources`, `Type` = Checklist.

## C1 · The Doom Pile Protocol
- **Use when:** "there's a pile of stuff I've been walking past for three weeks"
- **Topic:** Home · **Minutes:** 15 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> A doom pile is a pile that formed because every item in it requires a *decision*, and decisions are the expensive part. The trick isn't tidying faster — it's removing decisions from the process entirely. You will sort into four containers and you will not think about any individual object for more than two seconds.

**Before you start (2 min)**
- [ ] Set a timer for 15 minutes. **The timer is the whole method** — it converts an infinite task into a finite one.
- [ ] Get four containers: a bin bag, a "goes elsewhere in this home" bag, a box labelled **LATER**, and a flat surface for things that live here.
- [ ] Put something on in the background — music, a podcast, a show you've seen.

**The 15 minutes**
- [ ] Pick up one item. Two-second rule. One of four destinations, no third option:
  - **Bin** — rubbish, expired, broken, free with a magazine in 2019
  - **Elsewhere** — belongs in this home, different room. Goes in the bag; you are **not** walking it there now
  - **LATER box** — requires a decision, a phone call, or feelings. Anything sentimental goes straight here
  - **Here** — actually lives in this spot
- [ ] If you catch yourself holding something and thinking: **LATER box.** Holding = thinking = the thing that created the pile.
- [ ] Keep going until the timer goes.

**When the timer goes (3 min)**
- [ ] Stop. Even mid-pile. Especially mid-pile.
- [ ] Bin bag → actual bin, now. This is the only step you must finish.
- [ ] Elsewhere bag → by the door or on the stairs. Distribute whenever.
- [ ] LATER box → a cupboard. **Put a date on it 30 days out.** On that date, anything still in it goes, unopened. You will not miss it. (This is the single most useful part of the protocol.)

**What counts as a win:** one full bin bag. That's it. The pile not being gone is the expected outcome — doom piles are usually 3–4 sessions, and 4 sessions over a fortnight is a resolved doom pile.

**If 15 minutes is too much today:** do the **two-minute version** — stand up, fill one bin bag with only obvious rubbish, sit down. Genuinely counts.

## C2 · Pre-Appointment Checklist
- **Use when:** "I have an appointment and I always arrive flustered or forget the one thing I needed to say"
- **Topic:** Health · **Minutes:** 10 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> Two separate failures to prevent: the logistics (arriving, with the right things) and the content (saying the thing you actually needed to say). Most people only prepare for the first and then walk out of a medical appointment having forgotten the symptom that made them book it.

**The night before (5 min)**
- [ ] Confirm the time **and** the address — look at the actual booking, not your memory of it
- [ ] Travel time → **multiply by 1.5** → set a leaving alarm, not an arrival alarm
- [ ] Put by the door: ID, any letter or reference number, payment method, water, a snack
- [ ] **Write the three things you need to say or ask.** On paper or in your notes app. This is the step that matters most
- [ ] Write your one question you most need answered — put it **first**, in case the appointment gets cut short
- [ ] Set out what you're wearing (removes one morning decision)

**On the day**
- [ ] Alarm goes → leave within 10 minutes. Don't start anything. **Starting one small thing before leaving is the classic way to be late**
- [ ] Phone, keys, wallet, the paper, the reference number
- [ ] Headphones — waiting rooms are long and unpredictable
- [ ] Arrive 10 minutes early and sit outside. Early-and-waiting costs you nothing; late costs you the appointment

**In the room**
- [ ] Hand over or read from your paper. "I wrote this down because I lose the thread" is a completely normal sentence and nobody will find it strange
- [ ] Before you leave, ask: **"What happens next, and who does it?"** — the single most useful question in any appointment
- [ ] Write the answer down before you leave the building, not later

**What counts as a win:** you went, and you said the first of your three things. Everything after that is bonus.

## C3 · Taxes / Big Admin — Entry Point Only
- **Use when:** "I have to do my taxes and I physically cannot start"
- **Topic:** Money · **Minutes:** 20 · **Energy:** 🔋 Medium

> **This checklist does not do your taxes.** It gets you from frozen to started, which is the only hard part. The actual work is boring, and boring is manageable; it's the opaque, undefined, consequence-heavy shape that makes it unstartable.
>
> **Say this out loud first:** "I'm not doing my taxes today. I'm finding out what doing my taxes involves." Those are different tasks and only one of them is frightening.

**Phase 1 — Face it (10 min)**
- [ ] Timer: 10 minutes
- [ ] One new folder on your computer, or one physical envelope. Name it `[YEAR] TAX`
- [ ] Write a list — just the names — of every document you think you need. Don't find them. Just name them
- [ ] Next to each: `have it` / `know where it is` / **`no idea`**
- [ ] Count the `no idea` items. Usually 2–3. **That is the entire actual problem**, and it is much smaller than the feeling was
- [ ] Stop. Timer's done.

**Phase 2 — One `no idea` item (10 min, can be a different day)**
- [ ] Pick one. Not the hardest
- [ ] One action only: search an inbox, log into one portal, send one message to an accountant or employer
- [ ] If you hit a locked account, a lost password or a dead end: write down what's blocking it and **stop.** That blocker is now its own 2-minute task for another day
- [ ] Stop.

**Phase 3 — Only once the folder is full**
- [ ] Book 90 minutes. **Book it with another person present** — a body-doubling session, a friend on a call, a café. Do not attempt this alone; this is the single highest-value use of body doubling in the whole year
- [ ] Multiply your time estimate by 2.4
- [ ] Have the stop condition written down before you start: *submitted*, or *I know the one thing I need from someone else*

**What counts as a win:** Phase 1 done. You now know what the task is, and "unknown shape" was 80% of the dread.

**If there's a deadline and you've missed it:** the penalty for being late is almost always far smaller than the dread assumes, and nearly every tax authority has a process for this that they deal with constantly. Find out the actual number — the real figure is usually a relief, and "I don't know how bad it is" is costing you more than the fee.

## C4 · The 20-Minute Apartment Reset
- **Use when:** "the place has got away from me and I don't know where to start"
- **Topic:** Home · **Minutes:** 20 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> Not cleaning. **Resetting** — getting the place back to functional, which is a much lower bar and the only bar that matters today. Order is deliberate: you're buying back the *feeling* of control as fast as possible, not optimising for cleanliness.

- [ ] Timer: 20 minutes. Music on. Shoes on if that helps (it does, for a lot of people)
- [ ] **Minutes 0–5 — Surfaces, one room only.** Pick the room you sit in most. Everything that doesn't belong goes into one laundry basket. Don't distribute, don't sort. Basket.
- [ ] **Minutes 5–10 — All the dishes into the sink or the dishwasher.** Not washed. Collected. A kitchen with a full sink reads as dramatically better than a flat with dishes in four rooms
- [ ] **Minutes 10–13 — Bin round.** One bag, every room, rubbish only
- [ ] **Minutes 13–16 — Textiles.** All clothes into one pile or basket. Clean vs dirty is a decision and **today you are not making it**
- [ ] **Minutes 16–18 — The bed.** Pull the duvet flat. Thirty seconds, disproportionate psychological payoff
- [ ] **Minutes 18–20 — One flat surface, properly clear.** Table, desk, or counter. Completely clear. You need one piece of visible evidence
- [ ] **Stop.** Sit down in the room you did first.

**The basket:** everything you collected is still in a basket. That's fine. The basket is a legitimate storage solution for up to a week. Distribute it in a future 5-minute pass, or during the next reset.

**What counts as a win:** the bin bag and the one clear surface. If you only did those two, the place is measurably better and you did the thing.

## C5 · Packing Checklist (reusable, for any trip)
- **Use when:** "I'm going somewhere and I always forget something essential"
- **Topic:** Home · **Minutes:** 25 · **Energy:** 🔋 Medium

> **Pack the night before, not the morning of.** Morning packing with time pressure is how chargers get left behind. And pack in this order — it's by *consequence*, so if you run out of time or energy, the things you lose are the things you can buy at the destination.

**Tier 1 — cannot be replaced (do these first, always)**
- [ ] Medication — **count the days, then add two.** Then put it in your bag now, not later
- [ ] Any prescription letter/documentation, if crossing a border
- [ ] ID / passport / tickets — in the bag, not on the table
- [ ] Wallet, bank card
- [ ] Phone charger + cable ← **the single most-forgotten item.** Pack a spare cheap one permanently in your bag and never think about it again
- [ ] Keys (including the ones for when you get back)

**Tier 2 — annoying and expensive to replace**
- [ ] Glasses / contacts / solution
- [ ] Laptop + its charger (check it's the right one)
- [ ] Headphones
- [ ] Any specific thing this trip exists for — the gift, the laptop, the suit, the documents

**Tier 3 — replaceable at any shop, so stop worrying**
- [ ] Clothes: `days × 1` + one spare. Not three spares
- [ ] One warm layer, whatever the forecast says
- [ ] Toiletries, or accept you'll buy a toothbrush
- [ ] Shoes: the ones you're wearing plus one pair maximum

**The final sweep (2 min, by the door, bag already packed)**
- [ ] Say out loud: **phone, keys, wallet, meds, charger, ID.** Touch each one
- [ ] Charge your phone now, overnight
- [ ] Alarm for leaving, with travel time × 1.5
- [ ] Bins out, perishables dealt with, heating sorted

**What counts as a win:** Tier 1 complete. Everything in Tiers 2 and 3 is purchasable. A trip with your meds, your ID and a charger is a trip that works.

> **Make this permanent:** keep a small bag permanently containing a spare charger, a spare cable, earplugs, painkillers and a spare toothbrush. Never unpack it. It removes the same six decisions from every trip for the rest of your life.

## C6 · Medication Ran Out — Contingency
- **Use when:** "I've run out or I'm about to and I don't have a plan"
- **Topic:** Health · **Minutes:** 15 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **Not medical advice**, and nothing here is a recommendation about any medication — only about logistics and getting hold of the right people. Supply rules, prescription systems and controlled-drug restrictions vary hugely by country, so a local pharmacist is your most useful and most underrated resource here.
>
> **The bitter irony we should name:** reliably managing a repeat prescription requires exactly the executive function the medication helps with. Running out is a predictable system failure, not a personal one.

**Right now (today)**
- [ ] Count what you have left. Write the actual number down
- [ ] Work out your last day. Write the date down
- [ ] **Call the pharmacy first, before the doctor.** Ask two questions: *"Do you have my prescription?"* and *"Do you have stock?"* Supply shortages are common and the pharmacy knows about them before anyone else does
- [ ] If there's no prescription: contact the prescriber today, by whatever route is fastest. Say plainly: **"I run out on [date]."** A date gets action; "I need a repeat" goes in a queue
- [ ] If you'll have a gap: tell one person at work and one person at home that the next few days may be rough. That sentence costs very little and prevents a lot of confusion

**If there's a supply shortage**
- [ ] Ask the pharmacist to check other branches — many will phone round for you
- [ ] Ask your prescriber about alternatives. Do not change anything yourself, and don't buy anything from an unverified online source: counterfeit stimulants are genuinely dangerous
- [ ] Ask for a shorter-interval prescription so you're not exposed to a single collection point failing

**Then, so this doesn't happen again (10 min, once)**
- [ ] Set a recurring reminder **at 7 days remaining**, not when you're out. Title it with the action: *"Order meds — call pharmacy"*
- [ ] Switch to pharmacy auto-ordering/repeat dispensing if it exists where you are
- [ ] Put the pharmacy's number in your phone as a contact, so there's no searching involved
- [ ] Write the reorder steps down in your Desk as a 3-step routine. **Never rely on remembering a process you do once a month**

**What counts as a win:** you know your last day and you've spoken to one person about it. That's the whole of today's job.

## C7 · Job Application Checklist
- **Use when:** "I want to apply for a job and I have 14 tabs open and have done nothing"
- **Topic:** Work · **Minutes:** 45 · **Energy:** ⚡ High

> Applying has two parts that need separating, because mixing them is what creates the 14 tabs: **deciding** which jobs, and **applying** to them. Searching is novel and mildly rewarding; applying is boring and scary. If you do them in the same session, you will do the first one forever.

**Session 1 — Decide only (20 min, no applying)**
- [ ] Timer: 20 min
- [ ] Shortlist **three** jobs. Three. Not eleven
- [ ] For each, write one line: *why me, in one sentence*
- [ ] Delete every other tab. Genuinely close them — they're a dopamine source, not progress
- [ ] Stop. Do not start an application in this session, however keen you feel

**Session 2 — The assets (25 min, once, reusable forever)**
- [ ] One CV/resume. One. Current roles, dates, no gaps unexplained and no essay
- [ ] One cover letter **skeleton** with three gaps: `[role]`, `[company]`, `[the one specific reason]`
- [ ] A single document with: your references' details, your dates of employment, your qualifications, your right-to-work/ID details. **Every application asks for these and digging them out each time is where applications die**
- [ ] Save all of it in one folder called `APPLICATIONS`

**Session 3 — Apply to exactly one (30 min)**
- [ ] One job. The one you most want? **No — the second-most.** Do the one that matters least first, so the first form is the practice run
- [ ] Skeleton letter + the three gaps filled. 20 minutes maximum. **Nobody has ever been hired for the fourth draft of a cover letter**
- [ ] Submit
- [ ] Log it: company, role, date, in one place
- [ ] Stop for the day. One submitted application is a successful day

**Don't**
- ❌ Rewrite your CV from scratch for each job. Change two bullet points maximum
- ❌ Apply to eleven jobs on one good night. You'll write eleven weak applications and then not look for three weeks
- ❌ Research the company for 90 minutes. Fifteen minutes, their homepage and their about page

**What counts as a win:** Session 2 done. Those assets make every future application a 30-minute job instead of a four-hour one, forever.

## C8 · The Inbox Mercy Pass
- **Use when:** "my inbox has 4,000 emails and I've given up"
- **Topic:** Admin · **Minutes:** 30 · **Energy:** 🔋 Medium

> **You are not reading 4,000 emails.** You are never reading them. The goal is a usable inbox from today onwards, and the accepted cost is that some old things are lost. That's the right trade, and making it consciously is what ends the paralysis.

**Step 1 — The amnesty (5 min)**
- [ ] Select everything older than **30 days**
- [ ] Move it all to a folder called `OLD — before [today's date]`. **Not deleted.** It's searchable forever, which is the only way you were ever going to find anything in there anyway
- [ ] Inbox is now small. Sit with that for a second
- [ ] Say it out loud: *anything important in there will come back round.* It will. Important things repeat themselves — that's what makes them important

**Step 2 — Triage what's left (15 min)**
- [ ] Timer: 15 min. Three destinations, no fourth:
  - **Needs a reply** → a folder called `REPLY` (do not reply now)
  - **Needs an action** → the action goes in your task manager, the email goes to `OLD`
  - **Everything else** → archive
- [ ] Anything from a person who is waiting on you goes to `REPLY` and nowhere else

**Step 3 — Clear REPLY with holding replies (10 min)**
- [ ] Use script **S5** — *"Sorry for the slow reply. [answer]."*
- [ ] Target: as many as you can in 10 minutes. Do not craft. **Done is 10 minutes, not zero remaining**

**Step 4 — Stop it refilling (5 min, highest value per minute in this whole document)**
- [ ] Unsubscribe from the next five marketing emails you see. Not a bulk purge — five, as they arrive, forever
- [ ] Turn off every notification except from real humans
- [ ] One filter: anything with "unsubscribe" in it skips the inbox entirely. This one rule removes most of the volume permanently

**What counts as a win:** Step 1. The amnesty alone converts an abandoned inbox into a working one, and it takes five minutes.

> **Run this monthly**, and the 30-day amnesty becomes routine maintenance instead of an emergency. Put it in your Weekly Review's monthly slot.
