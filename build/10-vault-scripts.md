# 10 — Resource Vault: The 9 Scripts

**How these work in the product.** Each becomes one row in `DB: Resources` with `Type` = Script. The **Use when** line is the searchable field and must stay in the member's voice — that's the field they scan when they're in trouble. Content goes in the page body.

**The rule that makes these different from every other "email template" product:** every script is *send-ready*. No brackets to agonise over beyond a name and a date, no "customise to your situation," no advice about how to write it. A member in avoidance cannot do creative writing — they can copy, swap two words, and press send.

**The second rule: never explain more than you have to.** Over-explaining is the ADHD failure mode in every one of these situations. Each script has a "don't add this" note, and that note is often the most valuable line on the page.

## S1 · Ask for a deadline extension
- **Use when:** "I'm not going to make a deadline and I've been avoiding saying so"
- **Topic:** Work · **Minutes:** 3 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **Send it before the deadline, not after.** An extension asked for in advance is a logistics message. The same words after the deadline become an apology, and apologies cost you credibility that logistics don't. If you're reading this and the deadline has passed, use S2 instead.

**The standard version**
```
Hi [Name],

Quick heads up on [thing] — I'm not going to have it to you by [original date].
I can get it to you by [new date, end of day].

If that date doesn't work, let me know and I'll tell you what I can get you by
[original date] instead — it'd be [the partial version].

Sorry for the moved goalpost.
[You]
```

**The short version (someone you work with daily)**
```
[Thing] is going to slip to [new date] — flagging now rather than later. Shout
if that's a problem.
```

**The version where you need more than a couple of days**
```
Hi [Name],

I need to be straight with you about [thing]: my [original date] estimate was
wrong, and pretending otherwise just moves the problem later. Realistically it's
[new date].

Two options, your call:
1. [New date] for the full thing.
2. [Original date] for [the reduced scope], and the rest by [new date].

I'd pick 2 if [person/team] is blocked on this. Let me know which you want.
[You]
```

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ *Why* you're late. "I've had a lot on" / "things have been chaotic" invites questions and makes it sound like a pattern.
- ❌ An apology longer than one line. Long apologies read as unreliability; short ones read as professionalism.
- ❌ A new date you're not confident about. **Pick your honest date and multiply by 1.5.** Asking twice is far more expensive than asking once for longer.
- ❌ "Sorry, I know this is annoying, I feel terrible about it" — this is for you, not them, and it makes them manage your feelings.

## S2 · Tell your boss you missed something
- **Use when:** "I dropped the ball and I have to tell someone before they find out"
- **Topic:** Work · **Minutes:** 4 · **Energy:** 🔋 Medium

> **The whole game is being the one who tells them.** The gap between "they heard it from you" and "they found out" is the entire difference in how this lands. Send within an hour of noticing. The dread will tell you to wait until you have a fix — don't; waiting is what turns a small miss into a trust problem.

**The standard version**
```
Hi [Name],

I missed [thing]. Flagging it myself before it surfaces elsewhere.

What happened: [one factual sentence. No reasons.]
Impact: [who/what is affected, honestly]
What I've done since: [the step you took, even if it's small]
What I need from you: [a decision / nothing, just visibility / cover with X]

Happy to talk it through whenever.
[You]
```

**Worked example (so a member can see the shape)**
```
Hi Sam,

I missed the client invoice run on Friday. Flagging it myself before it surfaces
elsewhere.

What happened: the invoices for March weren't sent — I had them drafted and
didn't hit send.
Impact: four clients are a week behind on payment; nothing is overdue yet.
What I've done since: all four went out this morning with a short apology note.
What I need from you: nothing, unless you'd rather tell Finance yourself.

Happy to talk it through whenever.
Jo
```

**If it's serious and you want to pre-empt the obvious question**
Add one line, and only one:
```
To stop it happening again I've [the concrete mechanical change — a calendar
block, a checklist, a recurring reminder]. Not a motivation thing, a process
thing.
```

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ "I'm so sorry, I feel awful, I don't know what happened." You do know what happened, and this makes them reassure you instead of solving the problem.
- ❌ Your diagnosis. Not here, not in this message. See S4 for that conversation, held separately and on a good day.
- ❌ A promise it'll never happen again. You can't honour it and both of you know it. Promise a mechanism instead.
- ❌ Three paragraphs of context. Length signals guilt. Six lines signals competence.

## S3 · Cancel plans without lying
- **Use when:** "I can't face going and I'm about to invent a fake illness"
- **Topic:** Relationships · **Minutes:** 2 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **Why not just lie?** Because the lie requires maintenance — remembering it, dodging the follow-up, carrying the small shame. Honest cancelling is cheaper and, with people who matter, builds something the lie erodes. These are all true sentences you are allowed to say.

**The honest, low-detail version (use this one 90% of the time)**
```
I'm going to have to bail on tonight — I'm running on empty and I'd be bad
company. Sorry for the late notice. Can we do [specific alternative] instead?
```

**The one for someone who knows about your brain**
```
I've hit the wall today and I know if I come I'll be miserable and then
miserable about being miserable. Can we move it?
```

**Late notice, and you feel awful about the timing**
```
Cancelling this late is rubbish of me and I'm sorry. I'm not up to it tonight.
Say yes or no to [alternative] whenever — no rush and no hard feelings if the
answer's no.
```

**The one where you've cancelled on this person before**
```
I'm aware this is the second time and I don't want to become the person who
always cancels. I can't do tonight. Rather than suggest another date I'll
probably bail on: can you pick a day in the next two weeks and I'll work around
it? I'd rather commit to yours than keep moving mine.
```

**Not going to a thing you never wanted to go to**
```
I'm going to pass on [thing] — thanks for including me though, genuinely.
```

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ A fake illness. One, people can tell. Two, you'll have to remember it.
- ❌ A reason longer than one clause. Detail invites negotiation ("we'll have an early night!").
- ❌ "I'm the worst, I'm so sorry, I always do this, you must hate me." You've now made them comfort you about cancelling on them.
- ❌ An alternative date you know you won't make. Offer no date rather than a fake one.

## S4 · Ask for a workplace accommodation
- **Use when:** "I need something changed at work and I don't know how to ask without it being a whole thing"
- **Topic:** Work · **Minutes:** 10 · **Energy:** ⚡ High

> **Write this on a good day, send it on a good day.** It's the only script here that needs real energy, so don't draft it while you're struggling.
>
> **Lead with the business case, never the diagnosis.** Most accommodations are small and get approved on the spot when framed as "this makes me better at my job." You are not required to disclose a diagnosis to ask for a change in working arrangements, and in many places you have legal protection if you do — but how much to disclose is genuinely your call, and it depends on your workplace, your manager and your country. If the stakes are high, talk to your HR team, a union rep, or a local disability-employment service before sending. Not legal advice.

**The ask-for-a-change version (no disclosure)**
```
Hi [Name],

Can I ask for a small change to how I work? Three things, and all of them are
about output rather than preference.

1. [Specific change]. This would help because [concrete work benefit].
2. [Specific change]. Same reason: [benefit].
3. [Specific change].

I've tried [what you've already tried] and it helps but isn't enough on its own.

None of this changes what I deliver — it's about removing friction that's
currently costing me [specific hours/quality]. Happy to trial it for a month and
review.
[You]
```

**The version where you do disclose**
```
Hi [Name],

I want to tell you something and then be practical about it.

I have ADHD [diagnosed / in the process of being assessed]. I'm telling you
because a few small adjustments would make a real difference to my work, and I'd
rather ask directly than keep working around it.

What would help most:
1. [Specific change] — because [concrete benefit]
2. [Specific change] — because [concrete benefit]
3. [Specific change] — because [concrete benefit]

What this doesn't change: [the things you're already good at — name two]. This
isn't a performance conversation from my side; it's a "make the good bits more
reliable" conversation.

Can we trial these for a month and review?
[You]
```

**The accommodations that get approved most easily** (pick two or three, not eight)
- Written follow-up after verbal instructions ("can you drop the actions in Slack after we talk?")
- Agendas circulated before meetings
- Deadlines given as dates, not "when you get a chance"
- Noise-cancelling headphones, no explanation required
- A consistent desk / a quieter desk
- Core hours instead of fixed hours, or a later start
- Camera-optional meetings
- Fewer, longer check-ins instead of constant ad-hoc interruption
- Tasks in writing in one place rather than across four channels

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ Your medical history, your medication, or your diagnosis journey. Not relevant to the ask.
- ❌ An apology for existing. No "I know this is a lot" or "sorry to be difficult."
- ❌ More than three asks. Three gets approved; eight gets escalated to a process.
- ❌ Anything you can't demonstrate a work benefit for.

## S5 · Reply to an email you've been avoiding for weeks
- **Use when:** "I've been avoiding an email for 9 days and it's getting worse"
- **Topic:** Admin · **Minutes:** 2 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **The thing you need to know:** the recipient has thought about this for roughly four seconds total. The nine days of mounting horror happened entirely inside your head. Nobody is keeping the ledger you think they're keeping.
>
> **Never explain the delay.** Not one word about why. Explanation is the trap — it's what's kept you from sending for nine days, because there's no good explanation and you know it. Acknowledge, deliver, move on.

**The 15-second version (works for almost everything)**
```
Sorry for the slow reply. [The answer.]
```
That's it. That's the whole email. Two sentences has ended more avoidance spirals than any other line in this Vault.

**Weeks late, and you need more time still**
```
Apologies for the delay on this. Still relevant? If so I'll come back to you
properly by [day].
```

**You have no idea what they wanted any more**
```
Coming back to this embarrassingly late — can you remind me where we landed? Happy
to pick it up from there.
```

**You're not going to do the thing they asked**
```
Sorry for the slow reply, and sorry for the answer: I'm not going to be able to
take this on. Wanted to tell you properly rather than keep going quiet.
```

**It's now too late to matter**
```
Replying far too late to be useful on this one — apologies. If it comes round
again, I'm interested.
```

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ "Sorry, things have been mad / I've been swamped / this got buried." Every one of these is a flag you don't need to raise.
- ❌ Any mention of how long it's been. They may genuinely not have noticed. Don't tell them.
- ❌ A promise to be better at email. Nobody believes it, including you.
- ❌ A long reply to make up for the delay. Late *and* long reads worse than late and short. **Short is the apology.**

## S6 · Ask your doctor for an ADHD assessment referral
- **Use when:** "I want to get assessed and I'm scared of being dismissed"
- **Topic:** Health · **Minutes:** 15 · **Energy:** ⚡ High

> **Not medical advice** — this is a communication script, nothing more. How assessment and referral work varies enormously by country and healthcare system; a local ADHD charity or advocacy organisation will have far better specifics than we can.
>
> **Two things make these appointments go better:** bringing it in writing, and leading with lifelong functional impact rather than a request for medication. Write your notes beforehand, on a good day. Hand the paper over if speaking gets hard — that's a completely legitimate thing to do, and "I've written it down because I lose the thread when I'm nervous" is itself useful clinical information.

**What to say in the first 30 seconds**
```
"I'd like to be assessed for ADHD. I've written down why, because I know I'll
lose the thread. Can I give you this?"
```

**The one-page note to bring** (fill in before you go)
```
Why I'm here
I think I have ADHD and I'd like to be referred for an assessment.

How it affects my life now — specific, not general:
· Work: [missed deadlines / lost jobs / under-performing relative to ability]
· Admin: [unopened post, unpaid bills despite having the money, missed renewals]
· Home: [specific, e.g. "I've started the same tidying task 40 times"]
· Relationships: [forgotten plans, unanswered messages, conflict about it]
· Money: [late fees, duplicate purchases, unused subscriptions — a number if you
  have one]
· Health: [missed appointments, meals skipped, sleep]

Since when: [as far back as you can honestly go — school reports, childhood
reports, "I was always the one who…"]

What I've already tried: [planners, apps, lists, alarms, therapy] and what
happened: [worked for N weeks, then stopped]

What I'm asking for today: a referral for assessment. I'm not asking for
medication today.
```

**If you're brushed off**
```
"I understand. Can you note in my record that I asked about ADHD assessment
today, and tell me what the next step would be if I wanted to pursue it — a
different referral route, or a second opinion?"
```
Asking for it to be *recorded* is the single most effective sentence in the appointment, and it's entirely reasonable.

**If you're told "you can't have ADHD, you went to university / have a job / are a woman / aren't hyperactive"**
```
"I'd still like to be assessed. I've spent a lot of energy compensating, and
that's part of what I want to talk about. Can we make the referral and let the
assessment decide?"
```

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ A self-diagnosis delivered as a conclusion. "I have ADHD" lands worse than "I'd like to be assessed for ADHD" — same ask, very different reception.
- ❌ Internet research printouts or TikToks. Bring *your own* functional history instead; it's far stronger evidence.
- ❌ A medication request in the first appointment.
- ❌ Minimising. The instinct to say "it's probably nothing, I'm fine really, sorry to waste your time" is strong and it will cost you the referral. Write the note so you don't have to improvise against that instinct.

## S7 · Set a boundary with a parent (or anyone who knew you as a kid)
- **Use when:** "my mum/dad says something about my mess/job/life and I can't let it go"
- **Topic:** Relationships · **Minutes:** 8 · **Energy:** ⚡ High

> **Why this one is hard in a specific way:** for a lot of late-diagnosed adults, these are the people who formed the original narrative — lazy, wasted potential, so bright if only you applied yourself. A comment from them doesn't land in the present, it lands on thirty years of scar tissue. That's why the reaction feels disproportionate. It isn't disproportionate; it's cumulative.
>
> **Don't do this in the moment.** In the moment, use the holding line. Have the real conversation later, on a good day, ideally not in their house.

**The holding line, for the moment it happens**
```
"I don't want to get into this now. Can we talk about something else?"
```
Repeat verbatim if pushed. Do not improve it, don't justify it, don't win it. Repetition is what makes it work.

**The real conversation — the short version**
```
"I want to ask you for something. When you comment on [the mess / my job / my
plans], I know you mean well, and it lands really badly for me. I've spent most
of my life thinking I was failing at things that turn out to be genuinely harder
for my brain. I'd rather you ask me how it's going than tell me what I should
do. Can we try that?"
```

**If they get defensive ("I'm only trying to help")**
```
"I know. I'm not saying you did something wrong — I'm asking for something
different going forward. Those are different things."
```

**If they bring up the past ("you've always been like this")**
```
"Yeah. That's actually the point — it's been consistent my whole life, which is
why I now understand it differently. I'm not asking you to agree with me about
it. I'm asking you not to comment on it."
```

**If it's a repeated boundary they keep crossing**
```
"I've asked a few times now. If it comes up again I'm going to change the
subject, and if it keeps coming up I'll cut the call short. Not as a punishment
— I just can't keep having this conversation."
```
Then actually do it. An unenforced boundary teaches that boundaries are negotiable.

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ A diagnosis you have to defend. If they don't believe in ADHD, the conversation becomes about the evidence and you will lose, because you're not arguing about facts. Ask for a behaviour change, not agreement.
- ❌ Thirty years of receipts. Specific recent example, one, maximum.
- ❌ "You made me feel…" — reliably triggers defensiveness. Use "it lands badly for me" instead.
- ❌ This conversation at a family event, after drinks, or over text.

## S8 · Explain ADHD to a partner
- **Use when:** "my partner thinks I don't care and I don't know how to explain that I do"
- **Topic:** Relationships · **Minutes:** 20 · **Energy:** ⚡ High

> **The core misunderstanding to fix:** they're interpreting your behaviour as a statement about how much you care. Forgotten plans, unanswered messages, an unstarted task you promised — read as *priority*. For you it was never about priority. Naming that one mechanism resolves more conflict than any amount of apologising.
>
> **Pick a calm moment, not a conflict.** Never run this script in the middle of the argument it's meant to prevent.

**Opening it**
```
"Can I try to explain something about how my brain works? Not as an excuse — I
want to stop having the same argument."
```

**The three things to say** (in this order; the order does the work)

**1. Name the mechanism**
```
"When I don't do something I said I'd do, it isn't about how much I care. My
brain doesn't reliably convert intention into action, and it's worst with
things that are boring or have no deadline. That's not a feeling about you. It
happens with things I desperately want to do too."
```

**2. Name the cost to you** — this is the part they usually have no idea about
```
"The thing you probably don't see: I'm aware of it the whole time. The
undone thing sits there all day costing me energy. By the time you mention it,
I've already thought about it forty times. That's why I react badly — not
because you're wrong, but because you're confirming something I've been
hammering myself about since Tuesday."
```

**3. Ask for the specific thing, not for patience**
```
"Three things that would genuinely help:
· Ask me to do it now rather than later, if now is possible. 'Later' doesn't
  exist for me the way it does for you.
· Tell me things in writing — a text, a note on the fridge. Said out loud while
  I'm doing something else, it's gone.
· If something's not done, ask 'is this still happening?' rather than 'why
  haven't you done it?' Same information, and the first one doesn't put me on
  the floor.

And what I'll do: [one concrete, specific commitment — a shared calendar, a
Sunday admin hour, a weekly check-in]. Not 'I'll try harder' — that's what I've
been doing."
```

**If they say "everyone's a bit like that"**
```
"Probably, yeah. The difference is degree and cost. Most people forget a bill.
I've had [specific: three missed renewals, a lost deposit, a job]. It's the same
behaviour at a volume that changes outcomes."
```

**If they're hurt rather than angry**
```
"I know it looks like not caring. I'd read it the same way from the outside.
It's the thing I most wish I could show you from the inside."
```

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ A list of things that are therefore their job now. This is an explanation, not a transfer of responsibility — and if it reads like one, you'll lose them.
- ❌ "So you can't be annoyed about it any more." They can. Explanation isn't absolution.
- ❌ A diagnosis pamphlet or a video essay. Your own specific examples are far more persuasive than any resource.
- ❌ This conversation when either of you is already upset.

## S9 · Apologise without spiralling
- **Use when:** "I've apologised four times and made it worse and now I want to apologise for apologising"
- **Topic:** Self-talk · **Minutes:** 2 · **Energy:** 🪫 Low

> **What's actually happening:** for many of us, rejection sensitivity turns a small social error into a physical emergency. The over-apology is a nervous-system attempt to stop the feeling. But it transfers the discomfort to the other person, who now has to manage your distress about the thing you did to them — which makes the situation genuinely worse, which confirms the panic. That's the spiral.
>
> **The rule: one apology, one sentence, no follow-up.** A second apology is for you, not them.

**The whole script**
```
Sorry about [thing]. [One line on what you'll do instead, if anything.]
```

Worked examples:
```
Sorry I was short with you earlier — nothing to do with you.
```
```
Sorry about missing that. I'll get it to you by Thursday.
```
```
Sorry, I think I talked over you in there. Didn't mean to.
```

**If you've already over-apologised and want to close it out**
```
I'm aware I've apologised about four times about this, which is probably more
annoying than the original thing. Last word on it from me: sorry, and it's
sorted.
```
Then stop. Genuinely stop.

**For the spiral itself, before you send anything** — read these four lines:
1. They have thought about this for approximately four seconds.
2. The feeling in your chest is not evidence of the size of the problem.
3. A second apology makes it bigger, not smaller, because it tells them it was significant.
4. You can feel like this and send nothing. **The feeling is not an instruction.**

**Don't add this:**
- ❌ "I'm so sorry, I'm the worst, I totally understand if you're annoyed, I feel awful." Every clause asks for reassurance.
- ❌ An explanation of your brain. Not in an apology. That's a separate conversation on a separate day.
- ❌ A follow-up message checking whether they're still annoyed. This is the single move that turns a resolved thing back into an open thing.
- ❌ An apology for something nobody noticed. Pause and ask: *did anything actually happen?* About a third of the time, nothing happened.
